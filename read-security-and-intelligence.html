Read Security and Intelligence Reports

Follow the evidence through cyber notices, court records and declassified files.

By Edit USA · Reference guide · 3 min read

Read a security or intelligence story with two questions in mind: what does the available evidence establish, and who is making the assessment? A court filing, a technical advisory and a declassified memo have different purposes. Their claims need to be read in that context.

A document may be authentic while containing an unverified report, a disputed account or an early judgment that later changed. The institution holding a record is not necessarily the author of every statement inside it. Find the author, intended recipient and original date before treating it as a complete account.

Separate the event from its discovery

Cybersecurity timelines can include the first unauthorized access, discovery, containment, notification and public disclosure. A notice published today may concern an intrusion months earlier. A later update can expand the affected systems without showing that every customer experienced the same harm.

When a company says information may have been accessed, distinguish that from confirmed theft or publication. For your own account, read the notice for the product, version or affected period that applies. A general headline cannot determine whether your particular records were involved.

Read attribution as an assessment

A technical report can connect activity through infrastructure, code or behavior. That does not make every part of an operation's organization public. Check whether the author names an actor directly, says an actor is suspected or attributes activity to a group with government links.

Look for how the author reached the conclusion and what evidence is available to inspect. A report by the organization that discovered a problem is a primary account of that investigation. Independent confirmation requires additional evidence, not another website repeating the press release.

Keep court stages separate

What different records describe
RecordRead it for
Complaint or indictmentThe allegations and conduct prosecutors describe.
Plea or verdictThe charges resolved and what was admitted or found.
Sentencing recordThe penalty and the court's stated reasoning.
Agency history or memoirThe author's account, sources and perspective.

An accusation stays an accusation unless a later record establishes a different status. A decision on a procedural question also does not necessarily settle every factual dispute. Follow the record relevant to the exact claim you want to understand.

Find the original date of a declassified document

Declassification and online publication can happen long after a document was written. A new release may add evidence about a familiar case without describing a new operation. Keep the original date and release date separate when reading a timeline.

The National Archives' intelligence research guide describes records across agencies and collections. Its CIA records guide explains access limitations. A collection's boundaries help explain what you found and what may still be missing.

Leave redactions unresolved

A blacked-out passage establishes that material is withheld. It does not identify which explanation belongs in the blank. Look for a stated withholding category or a related released record without inventing a conversation or motive.

Historical espionage accounts can be gripping because of documented decisions and consequences. A person's private thoughts, a room's atmosphere or an exact exchange need evidence too. Distinguish contemporary records from later recollections when accounts disagree.

Read around a striking excerpt

The FBI Vault offers released files and collections. Open surrounding pages when a screenshot contains one dramatic paragraph. A header, attachment or correction can change how it should be understood.

For an active cyber incident, use the affected provider's current recovery instructions. Avoid running commands copied from an unfamiliar post or uploading sensitive account information to a site that claims it can tell whether you were affected. Follow the precise notice for the service you use.
