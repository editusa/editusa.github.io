How to Check a News Story

Find the source, put the dates in order and read the conditions that affect you.

By Edit USA · Reference guide · 3 min read

Before acting on a news story, find the document or announcement behind its central claim. Then check when it applies, where it applies and what stage the decision has reached. Those details can change whether a story affects you today, next year or at all.

A useful first question is specific: has an agency announced an idea, issued a proposal or adopted a final rule? A proposal describes a possible future requirement. An effective date in a final document answers a different question. The Federal Register reader aids explain document types; GovInfo provides background on its official Federal Register collection.

Follow the claim to a named record

Look for the agency, company, court or research group named in the story. Follow a link to the actual release, order or study when one is provided. A link to an organization's homepage leaves you with another search to do. Match the document title and date before assuming you have found the right record.

A primary source establishes what an organization said or did. It does not automatically settle a dispute about whether the organization is right. A company's description of a product is evidence about its announced features. Independent testing would answer whether those features work as promised. Keep the two kinds of evidence separate.

Put the dates in order

A story can contain several dates: the event, the announcement, publication, a correction and the day a change takes effect. Reading only the date beside the headline can hide that sequence. A newly published story may explain a rule adopted earlier; an older story may describe a deadline that has since moved.

Dates that answer different questions
DateWhat it tells you
Event or decisionWhen the development happened.
PublicationWhen the outlet first published its story.
Editorial updateWhen material reporting changed.
Effective dateWhen a requirement starts or an action is due.

For a deadline, the time zone belongs with the time. An application closing at midnight in Washington may close earlier on the West Coast. If the notice gives no clock time, do not assume that midnight in your own location is safe.

Read the geography and eligibility

A national company can announce a service available in only a few cities. A federal program can have eligibility limits. A state decision may affect residents, visitors and businesses differently. Find the affected group before applying a headline to your situation.

Imagine a hypothetical headline saying that a retailer now offers next-day shipping. The useful details are the qualifying ZIP codes, order cutoff, minimum purchase and excluded products. Without them, the headline describes a service but cannot tell you whether your order will qualify.

Inspect the comparison behind a number

When a story says that something rose, identify the starting point. A change from the previous month answers a different question from a change over a year. A percentage increase in a small sample may represent fewer additional cases than a smaller increase in a large population.

For company results, distinguish revenue from profit and a forecast from an actual result. For a survey, look for who was asked, how many people answered and when it ran. An anecdote can illustrate an experience without establishing how common it is.

Choose a source that answers your question

For an immediate local warning, go to the responsible local authority or National Weather Service office. For a regulatory change, read the operative document. For a disputed account, compare reporting that adds different evidence rather than several copies of one announcement.

You do not need to investigate every story. Spend the extra time when information affects a payment, a safety decision, an application or a plan that would be hard to undo. Keep the source link with a deadline you save so you can check it again when the decision is due.
